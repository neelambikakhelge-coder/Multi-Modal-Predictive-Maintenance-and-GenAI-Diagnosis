Section 4: Bearing and Vibration Diagnostics

4.1 Vibration Spike Root Causes

A sudden rise in vibration amplitude on the fan or core shaft bearings is most commonly
caused by bearing wear, imbalance from blade erosion, or misalignment following a hard
landing or foreign object damage.

Bearing wear typically presents as a gradual rise in RMS vibration over dozens of
operating cycles, whereas imbalance from blade damage tends to appear suddenly and
remains constant in amplitude at a fixed rotational frequency.

4.2 Recommended Action for Vibration Anomalies

When vibration sensors report values exceeding baseline by more than 30%, technicians
should first cross-check temperature sensors on the same shaft.

A simultaneous rise in both vibration and bearing temperature strongly indicates
lubrication breakdown or bearing surface wear.

Isolated vibration rise without temperature increase more often points to imbalance or
a loose mount.

In either case, a borescope inspection of the affected bearing housing is recommended
within the next maintenance window.


Section 5: Temperature and Thermal Degradation

5.1 Interpreting Gradual Temperature Rise

A slow, steady increase in exhaust gas temperature (EGT) or bearing temperature across
many operating cycles, without a corresponding vibration spike, usually indicates
gradual efficiency loss — commonly from compressor fouling, seal wear, or coolant
degradation.

This pattern differs from bearing failure, which pairs temperature rise with vibration
increase.

5.2 Recommended Action for Thermal Anomalies

If temperature trends upward by more than 5% over 20 cycles with no vibration
correlation, schedule a compressor wash and seal inspection.

If temperature rise is paired with rising vibration, treat as a bearing-wear case per
Section 4.2 and prioritize inspection.


Section 6: Remaining Useful Life (RUL) Interpretation

6.1 Using RUL Estimates for Maintenance Planning

An estimated RUL below 30 cycles should trigger a "Warning" maintenance flag,
prompting a detailed inspection at the next scheduled ground stop.

An estimated RUL below 15 cycles should trigger a "Critical" flag, and the asset should
be scheduled for inspection before its next operating cycle wherever possible.

RUL estimates should always be read alongside the anomaly detector output — a low RUL
combined with an active anomaly flag indicates higher urgency than a low RUL alone,
since it suggests an acute fault rather than gradual wear.

6.2 False Positive Considerations

Anomaly detectors can occasionally flag transient sensor noise as an anomaly,
particularly during rapid throttle changes.

A single-cycle anomaly flag with no corresponding trend in vibration or temperature
over the following 2–3 cycles can often be treated as noise rather than a genuine
fault, though it should still be logged for pattern tracking.


Section 7: General Maintenance Recommendations by Component

7.1 Bearings

Inspect for wear, pitting, or lubricant contamination.

Replace if vibration RMS exceeds 2x baseline or if bearing temperature exceeds
manufacturer threshold for more than 10 consecutive cycles.

7.2 Compressor Section

Wash to remove fouling if efficiency-related temperature rise is observed without
vibration correlation.

Inspect blades for erosion if vibration and temperature both trend upward slowly
over many cycles.

7.3 Sensors

Recalibrate or replace sensors that show implausible single-cycle jumps inconsistent
with surrounding readings, as these are more likely instrumentation faults than
mechanical faults.


Section 8: Compressor Fault Diagnostics

8.1 Compressor Fouling

Compressor fouling may cause gradual efficiency loss and a steady increase in
temperature-related sensor readings without a corresponding sharp vibration spike.

When this pattern continues across multiple operating cycles, inspect compressor
performance indicators and schedule a compressor wash during the next suitable
maintenance window.

8.2 Compressor Blade Erosion

Blade erosion can reduce compressor efficiency and may produce a gradual increase in
temperature together with slowly increasing vibration.

If both temperature and vibration trend upward over several cycles, inspect the
compressor blades for erosion, surface damage, or foreign object damage.

8.3 Compressor Surge or Instability

Rapid changes in pressure-related measurements accompanied by abnormal vibration,
temperature fluctuations, or unstable engine behavior may indicate compressor
instability.

Do not rely on a single sensor value. Cross-check related pressure, temperature,
and vibration channels before deciding on corrective maintenance.


Section 9: Electrical Fault Diagnostics

9.1 Electrical Sensor and Wiring Faults

Unexpected single-cycle jumps, drops to impossible values, or abrupt discontinuities
in an electrical sensor channel may indicate wiring problems, connector faults,
sensor failure, or intermittent instrumentation.

Before replacing a mechanical component, verify the sensor wiring, connectors,
power supply, and sensor calibration.

9.2 Power and Control Irregularities

Repeated abnormal readings across several electrical-related channels may indicate
a power supply or control-system problem.

Check electrical connections, grounding, connector condition, and control-system
diagnostics before returning the engine to service.

9.3 Recommended Action for Electrical Anomalies

If an electrical anomaly persists across multiple cycles, isolate the affected
sensor or circuit and compare its readings with neighboring sensors and historical
baseline behavior.

If the anomaly disappears after a connector or wiring inspection, log the event as
an instrumentation fault and continue monitoring.

If abnormal behavior continues after sensor and wiring checks, escalate the issue
for detailed electrical-system inspection.


Section 10: Diagnostic Decision Guidance

10.1 Combined Sensor Evidence

A single abnormal sensor value should not automatically be treated as a mechanical
failure.

Higher diagnostic confidence is obtained when multiple related measurements show
a consistent trend.

For example, increasing vibration together with increasing bearing temperature
is more significant than an isolated vibration spike.

10.2 Maintenance Priority

Use the following priority guidance:

Warning:
RUL below 30 cycles or a persistent abnormal trend requiring inspection planning.

Critical:
RUL below 15 cycles, especially when accompanied by an active anomaly.

Urgent:
Critical RUL combined with a persistent anomaly and supporting sensor trends.

Always review the machine health prediction, anomaly status, RUL estimate, and relevant
sensor trends together before making a maintenance decision.
